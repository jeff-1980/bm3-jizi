\title{Necessary but Not Transplantable: Deep-Noise Robustness of Mamba-Style Blocks Is a Component-Interaction Effect}

\author{Yi Wang\corref{cor1}}
\ead{jefflsxy@lsu.edu.cn}
\address{College of Engineering, Lishui University, Lishui 323000, Zhejiang, China}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author}

\begin{abstract}
The robustness of Mamba-style selective state-space models (SSMs) is widely attributed to their defining feature: input-dependent selectivity. We falsify this attribution through a three-step preregistered ablation chain on a controlled distribution-shift $\times$ noise testbed (bearing vibration signals under operating-condition drift). First, freezing selectivity---replacing input-dependent $\Delta$, $B$, $C$ with learned constants while keeping the block otherwise intact---costs only 2--5$\pp$ under deep noise, yet the frozen block still outperforms a parameter-matched time-invariant S4D by 14--18$\pp$. Second, component-wise ablation localises the necessary ingredient: removing the multiplicative SiLU gate collapses performance below plain S4D. Third, however, grafting the gate onto S4D recovers merely 17\% of the robustness gap---the gate is necessary within its native block but not transplantable. We conclude that deep-noise robustness is a component-interaction effect of the block composition, not a property of any single mechanism. Per-class analysis further shows the effect concentrates on the hard minority class, whose recall under noise is governed by gate presence. Our results caution against crediting input-selectivity for the matched-noise robustness of Mamba-style blocks, and against expecting any single transplanted component to confer that robustness on another backbone under a matched training budget.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Selective state-space models (SSMs) of the Mamba family have rapidly become default sequence backbones, and a common narrative has formed around \emph{why} they work: input-dependent selectivity---the ability to modulate state dynamics per token---is credited with filtering noise, adapting to distribution shift, and outperforming time-invariant predecessors such as S4D~\cite{gu2023mamba}. \cite{qu2024mambasurvey,patro2024mamba360} This belief motivates a growing family of ``selective'' architectures, yet recent evidence questions it: a systematic comparison on 59 clean multivariate time-series classification benchmarks (MONSTER and UEA) finds the \emph{simpler, time-invariant} S4D consistently ahead of Mamba-family variants in accuracy and efficiency~\cite{saadatmand2026s4d}, and theoretical analyses identify expressivity regimes where Mamba is provably weaker~\cite{yu2025blockbiased}. Which account is right---and, more importantly, \emph{what exactly should be credited} when a Mamba-style block does prove robust? This paper addresses the second question for one block and one testbed, with a controlled ablation chain whose decision thresholds were fixed before each grid was run. The testbed is bearing vibration diagnosis under operating-condition drift and matched noise, in which the robustness gap is large (up to 18$\pp$), the evaluation labels are identical across all 865 cells of the original grids (hash-verified), and the block can be dissected.

Our approach is deliberately \emph{attributional} rather than \emph{methodological}: we propose no new architecture and claim no state-of-the-art. Instead we take a regime in which a Mamba-style block exhibits a large, stable robustness advantage over a time-invariant SSM, and ask which ingredient earns it. The chain has three steps, each with adjudication thresholds fixed before the corresponding grid was run:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Test the classical selectivity terms (necessity test).} We construct a \emph{frozen} arm: the input-dependent parts of $\Delta$, $A$, $B$ and $C$ are replaced by learned constants. The Mamba-3 scan also takes an input-dependent trapezoidal weight and input-dependent rotation angles; these, the convolutional stem, the multiplicative SiLU gate, the state parameterisation, normalisation and projections are retained (Section~\ref{sec:protocol:freeze}). If these four terms drive robustness, the frozen arm should collapse toward the time-invariant baseline.
\item \textbf{Localise the necessary component.} On the frozen base, we remove one component at a time (convolutional stem; SiLU gate; heavy-tailed state parameterisation) under a single-variable discipline checked key-by-key on the state dictionary (Section~\ref{sec:protocol:freeze}), and adjudicate each arm against pre-specified $\Delta$-thresholds.
\item \textbf{Test sufficiency by constructive grafting.} The component identified as necessary is added to plain S4D as a gate branch, and a pre-specified recovery ratio decides whether ``removal collapses'' is matched by ``addition restores''.
\end{enumerate}

The outcome is a conditional pattern. Under independent training and evaluation noise (Section~\ref{sec:results:indep}), making the scan's dynamics and read/write coefficients input-independent does not remove the block's advantage over S4D ($+21.3$, $+23.3$, $+20.3$\,pp for the fully frozen block). Removing the gate costs $+9.4$, $+15.7$, $+27.0$\,pp in the partially frozen block, resolved only at $-6$\,dB, and without its gate the fully frozen block still exceeds S4D at $0$ and $-2$\,dB but not at $-6$\,dB: the gate's contribution grows with noise depth. Replacing the gate by an additive branch of identical size cannot be distinguished from removing it, in either host. Adding a gate to S4D, rebuilt in the donor's width, recovers $R = 0.30$ of the gap (paired-seed interval 0.14--0.72), not more than an additive branch with identical parameters ($0.41$) on the original transition, and somewhat more on the reverse one (Sections~\ref{sec:results:graftwm} and~\ref{sec:results:reverse}). On Paderborn the gate-removal cost replicates when training and evaluation share bearings and nothing replicates on disjoint bearings, where no architecture transfers well (Section~\ref{sec:results:pu}). A per-class analysis on a separate, shorter grid indicates that the gate-related differences concentrate on the minority class.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A layered freezing experiment on a Mamba-3 block under operating-condition shift and matched noise, from the classical selectivity terms ($\Delta$, $A$, $B$, $C$) to a scan whose dynamics and read/write coefficients are all input-independent, in which the block's deep-noise advantage over S4D survives every step, including under independent training and evaluation noise; each arm's frozen coefficients are verified input-independent at the kernel interface. (ii) Same-host and cross-host tests of the output gate: removal, in-place replacement by an additive branch of identical size, and grafting onto S4D with an additive control, showing a gate contribution that is resolved at the deepest noise level and an additive branch that does not substitute for it, while a time-invariant host gains little that is specific to gating. (iii) A correction of the noise protocol (training and evaluation noise originally shared by index) with a pre-specified re-run, and final-epoch reporting under a fixed budget with best-epoch selection as a sensitivity analysis. (iv) A documented pre-specification and review trail, including the incidents in which automated adjudication departed from the pre-specified criteria; we release the trail.

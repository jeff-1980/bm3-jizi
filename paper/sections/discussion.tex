\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What can be credited?} Within this Mamba-3 block, on this testbed and with noise-aware training, four statements are supported by independent-noise evidence. (i) Making the scan's dynamics and read/write coefficients input-independent did not remove the block's advantage over S4D on either transition of one dataset (the independent-noise grids contain no unfrozen BM3 arm, so they do not show that freezing preserves all of the unfrozen block's performance): S4D trails the frozen block by $12$--$28$\,pp. This does not show that input-dependence is useless: it shows that, in this block and under this budget, learned constants suffice to keep the advantage, and the two readings (``input-dependence contributes nothing here'' and ``what it contributes is recovered by constants'') lead to the same attribution. (ii) The gate-removal cost is large in the partially frozen block (resolved at $-6$\,dB on the original transition and at every level on the reverse one) and in the fully frozen block (resolved at $-6$\,dB). (iii) Without its gate the fully frozen block still exceeds S4D at $0$ and $-2$\,dB, so the gate is not required for a positive advantage there; the removal cost at those levels ($+5.4$ and $+9.3$\,pp) has intervals that include zero, so a contribution is neither shown nor excluded. At $-6$\,dB the gateless block falls below S4D. (iv) A multiplicative gate exceeds an additive branch of identical size and initial weights by $+23.3$\,pp at $-6$\,dB in the fully frozen block and by $+26.8$\,pp in the partially frozen block, whereas the additive arm cannot be distinguished from the gateless one; the pre-specified rescue test is nevertheless not assessable and equivalence is not claimed.

\textbf{Noise-dependent gating.} Only one setting shows a gate contribution that grows significantly with noise depth: the fully frozen block on the original transition (change of the removal cost between $0$ and $-6$\,dB $+27.2$\,pp, interval excluding zero; Section~\ref{sec:results:depth}). For the partially frozen block, the reverse transition and Paderborn shared bearings the data are compatible with a depth-independent cost, and the statement in the title is restricted to the first case. The depth analysis was added during review and is descriptive.

\textbf{Relation to earlier work.} Representability results for selectivity~\cite{huang2025selectivity} are not contradicted by a single ablation on one testbed. The S4D-superiority finding~\cite{saadatmand2026s4d} was obtained on clean classification benchmarks, without matched noise or a shift of operating condition, so it does not speak to the regime studied here. Two points of contact are nevertheless consistent with it: parity between S4D and the Mamba-style arms on clean data (\ref{app:earlier}), and the finding that input-dependent selectivity is not needed for the block's advantage. What this paper adds is specific to the matched-noise, cross-condition regime studied here, not a contradiction of the clean-benchmark result.

\textbf{Implications for backbone selection and for transplant practice.} For practitioners choosing backbones for noisy, drifting signal regimes, the results suggest judging the block as a composition. Whether a gate added to a different host reproduces its effect was tested only under the earlier noise protocol (\ref{sec:results:graftwm}): a gate built in the donor's shape recovered no more than an additive branch of the same size on the original transition and somewhat more on the reverse one, and neither design was tuned; we treat this as descriptive. A hypothesis for future work follows: a recomposed block guided by the component map (no conv stem, gate retained, S4D-style $A$) may match or exceed the original on this testbed; we leave it untested, because post-hoc recomposition after seeing the map would not carry the evidential status of the pre-specified chain.

\textbf{Why might gating be composition-bound?} We refrain from a mechanistic claim beyond the data, but note three compatible observations. First, the gate-related differences concentrate on the minority, lowest-recall class (\ref{sec:results:perclass}), and matched-noise training lifts that class \emph{only when the gate is present}. One hypothesis, which we did not test, is that the gate is the component through which matched-noise training improves hard-class recall, i.e., that it exploits noise-aware training rather than acting as a test-time denoiser; we measured neither classification margins nor gate activations, and the reading is in any case bounded by the matched-SNR scope (Section~\ref{sec:boundaries}). Second, the multiplicative form admits a per-sample suppression interpretation, but no gate statistics were recorded, so this remains a hypothesis rather than an observation. Third, the gateless composition falling to or below S4D at the deepest noise levels is compatible with the other components being calibrated to a gated regime (e.g., the stem may pass features that are only safe when the gate can veto them); this is the weakest of the three observations. The gateless--S4D comparison depends on host and protocol: in the fully frozen block under independent noise the gateless arm is above S4D at $0$ and $-2$\,dB and below it at $-6$\,dB (Table~\ref{tab:indep}), whereas for the partially frozen block under the earlier protocol it is not resolved against S4D with five seeds (\ref{sec:results:sensitivity}). All are testable in future work via pairwise interaction grids and mismatched-noise protocols.
